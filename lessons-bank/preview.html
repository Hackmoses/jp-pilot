What a wave carries

251 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
Drop a pebble into a water tank and watch a leaf near the side.
Idea
A wave is a disturbance that travels. It transfers energy while the material can move about its usual position.

Concept

waves-1-s1

Draw
A ripple crosses a tank in side view. A leaf bobs as the ripple passes.
Narration
Drop a small stone into a water tank. Watch the ripples spread towards the side. Pick a leaf floating near the edge and keep your eyes on it. The ripple reaches the leaf, lifts it, then moves on towards the wall.
  • ripples Reveal the pulse label above the advancing ripple. A disturbance spreads

waves-1-s2

Draw
A pulse travels along a rope. A marked knot moves up and down at a fixed horizontal position.
Narration
Now send a single flick down a washing line. Follow the knot we marked on the rope. The knot rises as the bump reaches it and comes back down after the bump leaves. The bump moves along the rope.
  • knot Emphasise the fixed guide through the knot as the bump approaches. Track one part of the rope

waves-1-s3

Draw
Show a moving bump and an energy arrow above the crest.
Narration
That moving disturbance is a wave. The hand supplies energy when it flicks the line. The disturbance passes that energy along the rope, so a part of the rope far from your hand can move even though your hand stays where it is.
  • energy Show the energy label travelling with the bump. The wave transfers energy

waves-1-s4

Draw
Replay the movement with a stationary horizontal reference through the knot.
Narration
It is easy to think the knot is carried along with the wave. Watch its position against the mark underneath it. It moves around its starting position as the disturbance travels past. The motion of the knot and the motion of the wave are different.
  • starting Highlight the knot’s vertical guide and the passing pulse. Local motion and travelling motion
Misconception
The knot travels along with the pulse.
Correction
The knot moves about its starting position while the pulse transfers energy along the rope.

Transverse and longitudinal waves

260 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
Imagine flicking a washing line, then pushing the end of a spring.
Idea
Compare how one part of the material vibrates with the direction in which the wave travels. This comparison gives the wave its name.

Concept

waves-2-s1

Draw
A wave travels right along a line while a marked point on it moves vertically.
Narration
Hold a washing line stretched out and flick your end up and down. The wave travels along the line towards the other end. Keep your eye on the marked spot. That spot moves up and down as the wave goes past.
  • spot Show the vertical motion arrow beside the moving marker. The spot moves up and down

waves-2-s2

Draw
Add a rightward travel arrow to the moving wave beside the vertical arrow.
Narration
Compare those directions. The wave travels to the right and the spot moves vertically. They are at right angles. We call this a transverse wave. Turn the picture on its side in your mind. The directions are still at right angles, so it is still transverse.
  • transverse Show both direction arrows together. Transverse · motion across the travel direction

waves-2-s3

Draw
Moving dots bunch and spread as the spring disturbance travels horizontally.
Narration
Now stretch a spring out on the ground. Push its end forwards and pull it back along its length. The crowded parts move along the spring. Each marked coil moves back and forth along that same line. We call this a longitudinal wave.
  • longitudinal Show a horizontal vibration arrow under the highlighted coil. Longitudinal · motion along the travel direction

waves-2-s4

Draw
Show the moving string and spring stacked with their travel and vibration arrows.
Narration
Both waves in the picture move to the right. Here is the difference to remember. On the string, the spot moves across the direction of travel. In the spring, the coil moves along it. Sound in air is longitudinal as well.
  • across Reveal the contrasting vibration arrows beside the two moving models. Compare the arrows for particle motion
Misconception
Waves moving in the same direction must be the same type.
Correction
Classify by the particle vibration relative to the direction of travel.

Amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period

267 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
Imagine a drummer at a school assembly. Watch what changes when the beat or the size of the vibration changes.
Idea
Amplitude is a displacement from rest. Wavelength measures distance, frequency counts cycles per second, and period measures time per cycle.

Concept

waves-3-s1

Draw
A wave oscillates. Measure from the rest line to a crest and then show the full crest-to-trough height.
Narration
Imagine a drummer at assembly. A stronger hit can make the skin move farther. On this wave drawing, the middle line is the rest position. The farthest displacement from that line is the amplitude. If you measure from top to bottom, you get twice the amplitude.
  • amplitude. Draw the amplitude bracket. Amplitude · rest position to crest
  • twice Add a second bracket showing the full height. Crest to trough = 2A

waves-3-s2

Draw
Follow two consecutive crests with a horizontal spacing bracket.
Narration
Now look along the rope in one snapshot. Start at a crest and go to the next crest. That horizontal distance is one wavelength. You could go from one trough to the next trough instead. For wavelength, use a matching point on the next cycle.
  • wavelength. Draw a horizontal bracket between adjacent crests. Wavelength · crest to next crest

waves-3-s3

Draw
A marker stays fixed while crests pass, then the source runs faster in a separate view.
Narration
Pick a spot and watch the crests pass it. Frequency says how many full cycles pass in one second. We measure it in hertz. Watch the marker as the source repeats faster. More crests now pass the spot in the same amount of time.
  • hertz. Show a bar timing the passage of a cycle. Frequency · cycles per second (Hz)
  • faster. Increase the rate at which crests pass. More cycles pass per second

waves-3-s4

Draw
A bar measures one oscillation of a marked spot.
Narration
Period asks a different question. How many seconds does one cycle take? Start the clock when the spot reaches the top and stop at the next top. A higher frequency means less time for each cycle. Divide one by the frequency to get the period.
  • clock Reveal the cycle timer under the marker. Period · seconds for one cycle (s)
  • Divide Replace the label with the reciprocal equation. T = 1/f
Formula
T = 1/f
T
period (s)
f

A

Misconception
Amplitude is the whole height from crest to trough.
Correction
For a symmetric wave the whole height is twice the amplitude measured from equilibrium.

The wave equation

254 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
Watch ripples pass a mark on the side of a water tank.
Idea
In one period a travelling wave advances one wavelength. Frequency tells you how many wavelengths pass in a second, so speed equals frequency times wavelength.

Concept

waves-4-s1

Draw
Show moving crests with the distance between adjacent crests bracketed.
Narration
Picture a water tank where ripples are passing a mark. Follow one crest while the next one approaches the mark. By the time the next crest reaches it, the first crest has travelled one wavelength. The time that took is one period.
  • wavelength. Draw the spacing bracket and track it with the crests. One period → one wavelength of travel

waves-4-s2

Draw
Track the crest and label the wavelength and the period relationship.
Narration
Speed is distance divided by time. For this wave, use the wavelength for distance and the period for the time. The frequency is one divided by the period. So the wave speed is also the frequency multiplied by the wavelength.
  • frequency Reveal the equation while the spacing bracket advances. v = λ/T = fλ

waves-4-s3

Draw
Move the wave under a label that combines the units for frequency and wavelength.
Narration
In the calculation, write frequency in hertz and wavelength in metres. Hertz means per second. Metres multiplied by per second give metres per second, the unit for speed. If your wavelength is given in centimetres, convert it to metres before using a speed in metres per second.
  • Hertz Show the per-second unit above the wave. Hz = s⁻¹
  • centimetres, Display the consistent units next to the bracket. m × s⁻¹ = m/s

waves-4-s4

Draw
Show a moving crest and a single marker while the requested unit changes to seconds.
Narration
A common wrong move is to multiply the two numbers in every wave question. First check which quantity the question asks for. If you want the period, you need seconds. Wavelength divided by speed leaves seconds because the metres cancel.
  • period, Show the equation beside the travelling wave. T = λ/v
  • cancel. Reveal the units with the metres cancelled. m ÷ (m/s) = s
Formula
v = fλ; T = λ/v
v
wave speed (m/s)
f
frequency (Hz)
λ
wavelength (m)
T
period (s)
Misconception
Multiply whatever two quantities are given.
Correction
Choose the relation whose result has the unit the question asks for.

Hook

Idea
Read what the question measures. Check which points repeat, convert the units, then pick the equation.

Concept

waves-5-s1

Draw
Send compressions and rarefactions past a fixed point.
Narration
A danfo horn sends sound through the air. Air particles move back and forth as the sound passes. Look at the dots in the drawing. They bunch in a compression and spread in a rarefaction. The crowded and spaced patterns move along towards your ear.
  • compression Reveal the horizontal motion arrow with the moving dots. Compression · crowded air

waves-5-s2

Draw
Draw a half wavelength from a crowded region to a spaced region, then extend it to the next crowded region.
Narration
JAMB can name two different points and ask for a wavelength. Start at a compression. The next rarefaction is halfway to the next compression. The distance you need for one full wavelength is between matching points, from compression to compression or rarefaction to rarefaction.
  • halfway Show the bracket across half a cycle. Adjacent compression to rarefaction = λ/2
  • matching Extend the bracket to the following compression. Compression to next compression = λ

waves-5-s3

Draw

Narration
Another trap is the unit. If the question gives centimetres for the gap but metres per second for speed, first work out what the gap means. Then express the full wavelength in metres. The frequency is the speed divided by that full wavelength.
  • metres. Show the converted wavelength unit beside the bracket. Use metres for λ when speed is in m/s
  • frequency Reveal the frequency equation. f = v/λ

waves-5-s4

Draw
Run both wave models with distinct motion arrows and a fixed spot for comparisons.
Narration
Some questions ask about directions. Compare the motion of the material with the direction of travel. Others give measurements, so decide whether you need a distance, a time or a rate. Write the unit you want before you choose a formula.
  • directions. Show both particle arrows. Direction question · compare arrows
  • unit Show the unit-check label. Calculation question · check the answer unit
Formula
λ = 2d; f = v/λ
λ
wavelength (m)
d

f

v

What is a mole?

261 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
At the market, eggs can be counted in trays. Chemists count tiny particles in groups too.
Idea
A mole is an amount that tells you the number of specified particles in a sample. One mole contains about 6.02 × 10²³ of those particles.

Review flag 2017 Q32 uses “a substance”. The display clarifies that atoms are the entities counted, as the answer does not generalise to the total atoms in a mole of molecules.

Concept

mole-1-s1

Draw
Egg-shaped counters drop into two trays in equal groups.
Narration
At the market, you can ask for eggs by the tray. A tray with a fixed number gives the seller a quicker way to count. In chemistry we use a much larger group because individual atoms and molecules are so small.
  • tray. Show equal rows of counters filling the two trays. Count in groups

mole-1-s2

Draw
The tray counters turn into moving particle groups labelled with a mole count.
Narration
That chemistry group is called a mole. One mole contains about six point zero two times ten to the power of twenty three particles. The number of particles per mole is called the Avogadro constant. The dots here stand for groups of particles.
  • mole. Show the rounded count alongside the two groups. 1 mol ≈ 6.02 × 10²³ particles

mole-1-s3

Draw
Atoms move into oxygen molecules and each whole oxygen molecule is counted once.
Narration
Always name the particle you are counting. A mole of oxygen atoms counts single oxygen atoms. A mole of oxygen molecules counts whole oxygen molecules. Each oxygen molecule contains two atoms, so that second group has twice as many oxygen atoms as the first group.
  • particle Bring the atoms together into pairs and highlight each whole molecule. Name the particle first
  • twice Show the two atom counts for each oxygen molecule. 1 mol O₂ molecules contains 2 mol O atoms

mole-1-s4

Draw
Two equal particle counts settle onto a scale with different mass labels.
Narration
A mole tells you how many particles you have. The mass of that group can change from one substance to another. Picture equal groups of light and heavy particles on a scale. Both groups have the same count, and the heavier particles give the larger mass.
  • mass Make the particles on the heavier side larger as the label changes. Same particle count, different mass
Formula
N = nNₐ
N
number of specified particles (count)
n
amount (mol)
Nₐ
Avogadro constant (≈ 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)
Misconception
A mole is a fixed mass in grams.
Correction
It is a fixed particle count. The mass depends on the substance.

Molar mass

262 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
A market seller can count two equal piles and still get different readings on the scale.
Idea
Molar mass is the mass per mole of a substance. Use the chemical formula to work out how much a mole of the specified particles weighs.

Concept

mole-2-s1

Draw
Equal groups of light and heavy counters move into view above a scale.
Narration
Imagine two equal piles at a market stall. One pile is made of light items and the other has heavier items. The piles contain the same number of items. If you put each pile on a scale, the mass can be different.
  • scale, Bring the two groups down towards the scale. Equal counts can have different masses

mole-2-s2

Draw
Gather the same number of dots into two boxes and label the mass of each group per mole.
Narration
Now make the groups one mole each. The mass of a mole is the molar mass. The unit is grams per mole. It answers a simple question. How many grams are in one mole of this particular substance?
  • grams Reveal the unit below the two groups. Molar mass · grams per mole (g/mol)

mole-2-s3

Draw
Join two small hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom and display the sum.
Narration
Read the formula for water. Each molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Using the rounded atomic values, add one for each hydrogen and sixteen for the oxygen. That gives a molar mass of eighteen grams per mole for water.
  • two Show two small dots joining the oxygen dot. H₂O · two H atoms and one O atom
  • eighteen Reveal the completed sum. M(H₂O) = (2 × 1 + 16) g/mol = 18 g/mol

mole-2-s4

Draw
Pair off oxygen atoms before revealing the molar mass of oxygen molecules.
Narration
A common slip is to read oxygen gas as single oxygen atoms. Its formula has two oxygen atoms in each molecule. Use the mass contribution of both atoms when you calculate the molar mass of oxygen gas. Check the small number in the formula before you add.
  • both Join matching oxygen atoms and show the sum. M(O₂) = 2 × 16 g/mol = 32 g/mol
Formula
M = m/n

m

n

Correction

Molar volume of a gas at s.t.p.

265 spoken words · 6 scenes

Hook
Picture two party balloons held at the same temperature and pressure.

Hook
A scale at the market gives mass, while a bottle label gives volume. Start with what the measurement actually tells you.
Idea

Narration
At a market stall, a scale gives a mass. A label on a bottle gives a volume. In a mole question, underline what you start with. If the question gives grams, divide by molar mass to reach moles. From there you can get a particle count.
  • grams, Move the marker through the conversion route. Grams → moles → particles

Draw
Advance the marker from moles to particles and then reverse to moles.
Narration
To get the number of particles from moles, multiply by the Avogadro constant. To go back, divide the count by that constant. Name your particle first. A count of oxygen atoms and a count of oxygen molecules answer different questions.
  • multiply Move the marker to the particles box. Moles × Nₐ → particle count
  • back, Move the marker back to the moles box. Particle count ÷ Nₐ → moles

mole-5-s3

Draw
Move the gas particles as the conditions switch from s.t.p. to a different pressure and temperature.
Narration
For a gas volume, look at the conditions first. At s t p, divide by the standard molar volume to get moles. At a different pressure or temperature, use the ideal gas equation. Write pressure times volume divided by the gas constant times absolute temperature.
  • conditions Mark the volume for the s.t.p. route. At s.t.p. · n = V/Vₘ
  • ideal Show the gas-law route. At other conditions · n = PV/(RT)

mole-5-s4

Draw
Show gas particles moving as the temperature readout changes from Celsius to kelvin.
Narration
A common mistake is to put the Celsius number straight into the gas equation. The equation needs kelvin. Add about two hundred and seventy three to the Celsius value. Check the units of the gas constant against the pressure and volume in the question.
  • kelvin. Switch the temperature readout to kelvin. T(K) ≈ t(°C) + 273
  • units Show the compatible units. atm · dm³ · K
Formula
n = PV/(RT)
n
amount (mol)
P

V

R
gas constant (0.08206 dm³·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)

Misconception
Use the s.t.p. shortcut or a Celsius temperature in any gas question.
Correction
At stated nonstandard conditions, use the gas law with temperature in kelvin.
