⚠ JAMB TRAP — ALUMINIUM AND PASSIVATION Aluminium is high in the reactivity series yet appears unreactive in practice. This is because Al reacts instantly with O₂ in air to form a thin, hard, impermeable layer of Al₂O₃ (aluminium oxide) that protects the surface from further attack. This is called passivation. Remove the oxide layer (with NaOH or amalgamation with mercury) and Al is extremely reactive. JAMB tests: "Why doesn't Al corrode easily despite being reactive?"
⚠ JAMB TRAP — IRON RUSTING REQUIRES BOTH O₂ AND H₂O Iron rusting requires the simultaneous presence of both oxygen AND water. Neither alone causes rusting. Rust = hydrated iron(III) oxide: Fe₂O₃·xH₂O. This is different from iron reacting with steam (Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂) which is a clean, reversible reaction.

Section 4

Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)

Industrial Production — Chlor-Alkali Process INDUSTRIAL

Electrolysis of Brine (Concentrated NaCl solution)
At cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ (H₂ gas produced)
At anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂↑ + 2e⁻ (Cl₂ gas produced)
Overall: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → Cl₂ + H₂ + 2NaOH

Three valuable products: Cl₂(g), H₂(g), and NaOH(aq). They must be kept separate — Cl₂ + NaOH → bleach; H₂ + Cl₂ = explosive!

Properties and Reactions of NaOH

  • White, waxy solid; highly soluble in water; strongly alkaline (pH ~14)
  • Deliquescent — absorbs moisture from air and dissolves
  • Reacts with acids: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
  • Reacts with amphoteric metals (Al, Zn, Pb): 2Al + 2NaOH + 2H₂O → 2NaAlO₂ + 3H₂↑ (unusual — most metals don't dissolve in alkali!)
  • Precipitates metal hydroxides: NaOH + metal salt → metal hydroxide ppt + sodium salt
  • Reacts with CO₂: NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O (excess NaOH); NaOH + CO₂ → NaHCO₃ (excess CO₂)
  • Saponification: NaOH + fat/oil → soap (sodium salt of fatty acid) + glycerol
  • Reacts with aluminium ions in water: Al³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Al(OH)₃↓; excess OH⁻: Al(OH)₃ + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻

Uses of NaOH

  • Manufacture of soap (saponification of fats/oils)
  • Paper and pulp industry (dissolves lignin)
  • Manufacture of rayon and artificial silk
  • Cleaning agent and drain unblocker (dissolves grease and aluminium)
  • Precipitation of metal hydroxides in qualitative analysis
  • Manufacture of detergents, textiles, dyes
⚠ JAMB TRAP — NaOH DISSOLVES ALUMINIUM, ZINC, AND LEAD IONS NaOH precipitates most metal hydroxides. But with Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, and Pb²⁺, excess NaOH dissolves the precipitate:
  • Al(OH)₃↓ + OH⁻ → [Al(OH)₄]⁻ (aluminate — dissolves, colourless)
  • Zn(OH)₂↓ + 2OH⁻ → [Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ (zincate — dissolves, colourless)
  • Pb(OH)₂↓ + 2OH⁻ → [Pb(OH)₄]²⁻ (plumbate — dissolves)
So: NaOH(aq) + Al³⁺: white precipitate forms, then dissolves in excess. NaOH(aq) + Fe³⁺: reddish-brown precipitate forms, does NOT dissolve in excess. This is a key distinction JAMB tests.

Sodium Carbonate & Sodium Hydrogencarbonate — Solvay Process

Solvay (Ammonia-Soda) Process INDUSTRIAL

Solvay Process — Making Na₂CO₃

Raw materials: NaCl (brine), CaCO₃ (limestone), NH₃ (recycled)

Step 1: CO₂ from limestone: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ [heat]
Step 2: Dissolve NH₃ in brine: NaCl(aq) + NH₃(g) → saturated ammoniated brine
Step 3: Pass CO₂ through: NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NH₄HCO₃
Step 4: NaHCO₃ precipitates: NaCl + NH₄HCO₃ → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl
Step 5: Filter and heat NaHCO₃: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ [heat]
Step 6: Recover NH₃: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + 2H₂O [ammonia recycled!]

The CaO from step 1 is slaked (CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂) for step 6. NH₃ is recycled — so the only true raw materials consumed are NaCl and CaCO₃.

Na₂CO₃ vs NaHCO₃ — Properties Compared

PropertyNa₂CO₃ (washing soda)NaHCO₃ (baking soda)
Action of heatStable to heat (no decomposition)2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ (decomposes ~50°C)
pH in solution~11 (strongly alkaline — CO₃²⁻ hydrolyses)~8.3 (mildly alkaline)
With acidNa₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑

Uses of Sodium Carbonate (Na₂CO₃)

  • Manufacture of glass (Na₂CO₃ + SiO₂ + CaCO₃ → soda-lime glass when heated)
  • Water softening (removes temporary and permanent hardness: Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃↓)
  • Manufacture of soap, detergents, paper
  • Cleaning agent — domestic soda crystals
  • Neutralising acid spills

Uses of Sodium Hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃)

  • Baking powder (releases CO₂ when heated or acidified → makes dough rise)
  • Antacid (neutralises excess stomach acid: NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂)
  • Fire extinguisher powder (CO₂ released smothers fire)
  • Manufacture of effervescent tablets and drinks

Sodium Chloride (NaCl) OCCURRENCE

  • Occurs in sea water (~3.5% by mass); in underground rock salt deposits (halite); brine springs
  • Recovered by: solar evaporation (in warm countries), solution mining (pump water into deposits, pump brine out), mining (rock salt)
  • Economic importance of seawater: NaCl, Mg (from MgCl₂), Br₂ (from bromides), KCl, CaCl₂, desalination for freshwater

Uses of Sodium Chloride

  • Food preservation and seasoning
  • Manufacture of NaOH, Cl₂, H₂ (chlor-alkali process)
  • Manufacture of Na₂CO₃ (Solvay process)
  • Gritting roads (lowers freezing point of water)
  • Manufacture of HCl (NaCl + H₂SO₄)
  • Preserving hides and fish

Cement — Composition and Setting

Cement — Composition Cement is made by heating a mixture of limestone (CaCO₃) and clay (containing SiO₂ and Al₂O₃) in a rotary kiln at ~1450°C. The product, called clinker, is ground with a small amount of gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) to make Portland cement. Main components: calcium silicates (Ca₃SiO₅, Ca₂SiO₄), calcium aluminates (Ca₃Al₂O₆).
Setting of Mortar Mortar = cement + sand + water. Setting (hardening) involves two stages: (1) Hydration — calcium silicates and aluminates react with water to form a rigid interlocking gel. (2) Carbonation — Ca(OH)₂ produced absorbs CO₂ from air: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (long-term hardening). Concrete = cement + sand + gravel. Reinforced concrete = concrete + steel rods.

Test for Ca²⁺ TEST

✅ CONFIRMATORY TEST FOR Ca²⁺

Flame test: Dip platinum wire in sample, hold in blue Bunsen flame.

Result: Brick-red/orange-red flame colour indicates Ca²⁺.

Precipitation test: Add Na₂SO₄(aq) or dilute H₂SO₄ to the solution.

Result: White precipitate of CaSO₄ (slightly soluble — may be slow to form, especially in cold dilute solutions).

OR add Na₂CO₃: Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → CaCO₃↓ (white ppt, soluble in dilute HCl).

⚠ JAMB TRAP — LIMEWATER AND EXCESS CO₂ Limewater + CO₂ → milky (CaCO₃ precipitates). But if you keep passing CO₂: CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂ (calcium hydrogencarbonate — soluble → milkiness clears). So: "milky then clears on excess CO₂" = calcium compound confirmed. This also explains temporary hardness in water (CaCO₃ dissolved by CO₂ → Ca(HCO₃)₂).

Aluminium — Extraction, Properties & Uses

Al
Aluminium
Group III · Atomic no. 13 · M = 27 g/mol · Light, silvery metal · Most abundant metal in Earth's crust

Ore and Purification — Bayer Process

Ore: Bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O, a hydrated aluminium oxide, also called gibbsite/diaspore) — impurities: Fe₂O₃ (red), SiO₂, TiO₂.

Bayer Process — Purification of Bauxite
Step 1 (Dissolve Al₂O₃): Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂(aq) + H₂O [bauxite dissolved in hot conc. NaOH — Al₂O₃ is amphoteric; Fe₂O₃ and SiO₂ impurities don't dissolve]
Step 2 (Filter): Fe₂O₃ (red mud) filtered off
Step 3 (Precipitate): NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O → Al(OH)₃↓ + NaOH [dilute and seed with Al(OH)₃ crystals]
Step 4 (Calcine): 2Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O [heat — gives pure alumina/corundum]

Extraction — Hall-Héroult Process INDUSTRIAL

Hall-Héroult Process — Electrolytic Extraction of Al

Electrolyte: Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) at ~960°C. Cryolite lowers the melting point of Al₂O₃ from ~2050°C to ~960°C (economical!).

At cathode (−): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al [liquid Al sinks to bottom, tapped off]
At anode (+): 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ [but carbon anodes burn in O₂: C + O₂ → CO₂; anodes must be replaced regularly]

The carbon anodes are consumed and must be periodically replaced. This is a significant operating cost. The steel cell is lined with graphite (cathode lining).

⚠ JAMB TRAP — WHY CRYOLITE? Pure Al₂O₃ melts at ~2050°C — too high to be practical for electrolysis. Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) is used as a solvent for Al₂O₃, reducing the operating temperature to ~960°C. This saves enormous amounts of energy and makes the process economical. JAMB often asks "why is cryolite used?" Answer: to lower the melting point (not to increase purity, not as an electrode).
⚠ JAMB TRAP — ANODES ARE CONSUMED IN Hall-Héroult In the Hall-Héroult process, the carbon (graphite) anodes are gradually consumed because the O₂ produced at the anode reacts with the hot carbon: C + O₂ → CO₂. This is why anodes must be regularly replaced. In contrast, in the electrolysis of copper, the anode is impure copper that dissolves but is "consumed" differently (it is electrochemically dissolved, not oxidised by O₂).

Test for Al³⁺ TEST

✅ CONFIRMATORY TEST FOR Al³⁺

Add NaOH(aq) to the solution.

Result: White gelatinous precipitate of Al(OH)₃ forms initially.

Key: Al(OH)₃ dissolves in excess NaOH → colourless solution of [Al(OH)₄]⁻ (aluminate).

This distinguishes Al³⁺ from Fe³⁺ (reddish-brown, does NOT dissolve in excess NaOH) and from Ca²⁺ (no precipitate with NaOH).

Flame test: Al³⁺ gives NO characteristic flame colour (colourless/dull).

Tin — Extraction & Uses

Sn
Tin
Group IV · Atomic no. 50 · M = 118.7 g/mol · Silvery-white, ductile metal

Ores of Tin

  • Cassiterite (tinstone) — SnO₂ (tin(IV) oxide) — main ore
  • Also: stannite (Cu₂FeSnS₄) — less common

Extraction of Tin — Smelting INDUSTRIAL

Ore crushed, washed, and concentrated (gravity separation — SnO₂ is dense)
SnO₂(s) + 2C(s) → Sn(l) + 2CO(g) [reduction by carbon/coke at ~1200°C in a reverberatory furnace]
Molten tin (MP = 232°C) flows to base; impurities removed by poling (stirring with green wood)

Note: Unlike iron, tin smelting uses a reverberatory furnace (not a blast furnace) because Sn is lower-melting and the process is simpler.

Key Uses of Tin

  • Tinplating (tinning) — coating steel food cans (corrosion protection for food storage)
  • Solder — Sn + Pb alloy (joins electrical connections)
  • Bronze — Cu + Sn alloy
  • Type metal — Sn + Pb + Sb alloy
  • Glass production — float glass process (molten glass floated on liquid tin)
⚠ JAMB TRAP — TINNING vs GALVANISING: WHICH IS SAFER WHEN SCRATCHED? Galvanising (zinc coating on steel): Zn is above Fe in reactivity series → Zn is sacrificed instead of Fe (cathodic/sacrificial protection). Even when scratched, Fe is protected. Tinning (tin coating on steel): Sn is below Fe in reactivity series → Fe is the anode, Sn is the cathode. When scratched, Fe corrodes faster than uncoated iron (galvanic corrosion accelerated). For food cans this is acceptable (Sn layer intact, content is acidic/anaerobic). But tinning is less safe than galvanising when the coating is broken.

Iron — Extraction, Forms & Uses

Fe
Iron
Group 8 (VIII B) · Atomic no. 26 · M = 56 g/mol · Most widely used metal · Config [Ar]3d⁶4s²

Ores of Iron

  • Haematite — Fe₂O₃ (main ore; rich, ~70% Fe)
  • Magnetite — Fe₃O₄ (magnetic iron ore)
  • Limonite — Fe₂O₃·3H₂O (hydrated iron(III) oxide)
  • Iron pyrite (fool's gold) — FeS₂ (sulphide ore; must be roasted to oxide first)
  • Siderite — FeCO₃ (iron carbonate)

Extraction — Blast Furnace INDUSTRIAL

Blast Furnace — Iron Extraction

Charge: Iron ore (haematite Fe₂O₃) + coke (C) + limestone (CaCO₃). Hot air blast from bottom.

Zone 1 (bottom, ~1900°C): C + O₂ → CO₂ [coke burns in hot air blast]
Zone 2 (just above): CO₂ + C → 2CO [CO₂ reduced to CO — this is the main reducing agent!]
Zone 3 (middle): Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ [iron ore reduced by CO]
Also: Fe₃O₄ + 4CO → 3Fe + 4CO₂; FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂
Zone 4 (limestone decomposes): CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ [at ~800°C]
Zone 5 (slag formation): CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ [calcium silicate — molten slag floats on iron]

Products: Molten iron (pig iron) tapped from the bottom; molten slag (CaSiO₃) tapped separately (used in road-making, cement); exhaust gas (mostly CO, CO₂, N₂) used as fuel to pre-heat the air blast.

⚠ JAMB TRAP — CO IS THE MAIN REDUCING AGENT IN BLAST FURNACE, NOT C Although coke (C) is added to the blast furnace, the actual reducing agent that reduces the iron ore is CO (carbon monoxide), not C directly. C → CO₂ → CO → reduces Fe₂O₃ → CO₂. Carbon directly reduces iron only at very high temperatures near the base. The key equation to remember: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
⚠ JAMB TRAP — ROLE OF LIMESTONE IN BLAST FURNACE Limestone (CaCO₃) is added as a flux. Its role is to remove silica (SiO₂) impurities from the ore as slag: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag). Without limestone, silica impurities would clog the furnace. Slag is also used as a by-product (road-making, cement).

Alloys — Constituents & Uses

Alloy An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually metals or carbon), made to improve properties such as hardness, strength, corrosion resistance, or appearance. Alloys are not compounds — they have no fixed composition and are mixed in the molten state.
Steel (carbon steel)
Fe + 0.1–1.5% C

Stronger and harder than pure iron. Used in construction, car bodies, tools, machinery, bridges.

Stainless Steel
Fe + ~11–18% Cr + ~8% Ni + trace C

Corrosion resistant (Cr₂O₃ layer). Cutlery, surgical instruments, kitchen equipment.

Brass
Cu + 20–45% Zn

Attractive gold colour; harder than Cu; more corrosion resistant than Zn. Musical instruments, coins, taps, screws, door fittings.

Bronze
Cu + 10–20% Sn (sometimes + small % Al or P)

Harder than brass; corrosion resistant; good for casting. Statues, bells, medals, bearings, springs, coins.

Duralumin
Al + 4% Cu + small amounts Mg and Mn

Light but strong — combines low density of Al with extra strength. Aircraft frames, bicycles, lightweight machinery.

Soft Solder
Sn + Pb (typically 60% Sn, 40% Pb; or 50:50)

Low melting point (180–230°C). Joining electrical components; plumbing. Lead-free solders now preferred (Sn + Cu/Ag).

Type Metal
Pb + Sn + Sb (antimony)

Expands slightly on solidifying → crisp print impression. Historically used for printing type in letterpress printing.

Permalloy
Fe + ~78–80% Ni

Highly magnetic (high magnetic permeability). Magnetic shielding, transformer cores, telephone cables.

Alnico
Fe + Al + Ni + Co (+ traces Cu, Ti)

Permanent magnet alloy. Loudspeakers, microphones, sensors, electric motors, guitar pickups.

Why Are Alloys Better Than Pure Metals?

  • Harder — foreign atoms disrupt the regular lattice, preventing layers from sliding (no easy dislocation movement)
  • Stronger — improved tensile strength
  • More corrosion resistant — e.g. Cr in stainless steel forms protective Cr₂O₃
  • Better appearance — e.g. brass is gold-coloured
  • Controlled melting point — e.g. solder has low MP for ease of use
  • Magnetic properties — e.g. alnico, permalloy
⚠ JAMB TRAP — ALLOY COMPOSITION MEMORY AID
  • Brass = Brass → Cu + Zn ("Cu-Zn brass")
  • Bronze = Cu + Sn ("Bronze Age = copper + tin")
  • Duralumin = Al + Cu (aircraft — dura = durable, Al = light)
  • Solder = Sn + Pb (low melting — tin + lead)
  • Stainless steel = Fe + Cr + Ni (stain-LESS = corrosion resistant due to Cr)
  • Alnico = Al + Ni + Co (spelled in the name: Al-Ni-Co)
  • Type metal = Pb + Sn + Sb (type = printing type)
  • Permalloy = Fe + Ni (PERMAnent magnet + alloy)

Section 16

⚡ Lightning Round — All Major JAMB Traps

⚡ Master Trap List — Topic 16
  • Reactivity series extraction rule: Very reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) → electrolysis of molten compound. Moderately reactive (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb) → reduction by carbon/CO. Unreactive (Ag, Au) → found free or simple heating.
  • Aluminium passivation: Al appears unreactive because a self-forming Al₂O₃ layer protects the surface. It is actually very reactive (high in series). Remove oxide layer with NaOH and Al reacts vigorously.
  • Rusting requires BOTH O₂ AND H₂O simultaneously. Neither alone causes rusting of iron.
  • Cryolite in Hall-Héroult: Used to lower melting point of Al₂O₃ from ~2050°C to ~960°C. NOT to purify Al, NOT as electrode material.
  • Carbon anodes in Hall-Héroult are consumed: O₂ from electrolysis burns them (C + O₂ → CO₂). They must be periodically replaced. This is a major operating cost.
  • Blast furnace reducing agent is CO, not C directly: C + O₂ → CO₂ → CO₂ + C → 2CO → CO reduces Fe₂O₃. The key reduction: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
  • Limestone in blast furnace = flux, not a fuel or reducing agent. It removes SiO₂ as slag CaSiO₃. Without it, silica clogs the furnace.
  • Al³⁺ and Zn²⁺ precipitates dissolve in excess NaOH (amphoteric hydroxides). Fe²⁺ (green), Fe³⁺ (rust-brown), Cu²⁺ (pale blue) precipitates do NOT dissolve in excess NaOH.
  • Fe²⁺ turns green ppt → brown on standing because Fe(OH)₂ is oxidised by air to Fe(OH)₃. So if you see green ppt turning brown → Fe²⁺ not Fe³⁺ was originally present.
  • KSCN test for Fe³⁺ gives blood-red [Fe(SCN)]²⁺. K₃[Fe(CN)₆] + Fe²⁺ → Turnbull's blue. K₄[Fe(CN)₆] + Fe³⁺ → Prussian blue. Don't mix up these reagents!
  • Cu²⁺ + excess NH₃ → deep blue complex [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺. This dissolves the pale blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate. This is different from excess NaOH (which does NOT dissolve Cu(OH)₂).
  • Steel vs cast iron: Steel is less brittle, stronger in tension, can be welded. Cast iron is very hard but brittle. The difference is carbon content.
  • Galvanising (Zn) protects Fe even when scratched — sacrificial protection. Tinning (Sn) — Fe corrodes faster when scratched because Fe is above Sn in reactivity.
  • Transition metal 4s electrons lost first: Fe → Fe²⁺: loses 2 × 4s → [Ar]3d⁶. Fe³⁺: loses 2 × 4s + 1 × 3d → [Ar]3d⁵. NEVER [Ar]3d⁴4s².
  • Cr and Cu anomalous configs: Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ (half-filled 3d); Cu = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ (full 3d). Both more stable than the "expected" config.
  • Solvay process — only NaCl and CaCO₃ are consumed. NH₃ is recycled. NaHCO₃ precipitates (not Na₂CO₃) because NaHCO₃ is less soluble. Then heated to give Na₂CO₃.
  • Na₂CO₃ uses: glass making (with SiO₂ + CaCO₃), water softening, soap/detergent manufacture. NaHCO₃ uses: baking (CO₂ release), antacid, fire extinguisher.
  • CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat (slaking). This is highly exothermic — can cause burns.
  • Limewater + CO₂ → milky (CaCO₃). Excess CO₂ → milkiness clears (Ca(HCO₃)₂ forms — soluble). "Milky then clears" = hallmark of calcium chemistry with CO₂.
  • Alloy compositions to memorise: Brass = Cu+Zn; Bronze = Cu+Sn; Duralumin = Al+Cu; Solder = Sn+Pb; Type metal = Pb+Sn+Sb; Alnico = Fe+Al+Ni+Co; Permalloy = Fe+Ni; Stainless = Fe+Cr+Ni.
  • Anhydrous CuSO₄ = white → blue with water: test for water. CuSO₄·5H₂O = blue vitriol.
  • In electrolytic refining of Cu: impure Cu anode dissolves; pure Cu deposits at cathode. Noble metals (Au, Ag) sink as anode sludge. Less noble impurities (Fe, Zn, Ni) stay in solution.
  • Na reacts with cold water vigorously: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ (fizzes, skims, may catch fire — lilac flame). Ca reacts more slowly and steadily. K is more violent than Na.
  • Amphoteric metals Al, Zn, Pb react with NaOH to give H₂: 2Al + 2NaOH + 2H₂O → 2NaAlO₂ + 3H₂↑. Most metals do NOT react with NaOH.
  • Thermite reaction — Al reduces Fe₂O₃: 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe (extremely exothermic, ~2500°C). Used to weld railway tracks. Demonstrates Al is more reactive than Fe.

Key Definitions & Self-Test

Alloy
A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (metals or non-metals) to improve properties; no fixed composition; made by mixing in molten state.
Passivation
Formation of a thin, adherent, impermeable oxide layer on a metal surface that prevents further corrosion; e.g. Al₂O₃ on aluminium, Cr₂O₃ on chromium/stainless steel.
Flux (blast furnace)
Limestone (CaCO₃) added to the blast furnace; decomposes to CaO which reacts with silica impurities to form molten slag (CaSiO₃), allowing it to be removed.
Slag
Calcium silicate (CaSiO₃) — waste product from the blast furnace; floats on molten iron and is tapped off separately. Used in road-making and cement.
Electrolytic refining
Process of purifying an impure metal using electrolysis; impure metal is the anode (dissolves), pure metal deposits at cathode; used for Cu, Al (from electrolytic extraction).
Sacrificial protection
Corrosion protection by connecting the metal to a more reactive metal (e.g. Zn block on ship hull); the reactive metal corrodes preferentially ("sacrificed"), protecting the less reactive metal.
Galvanising
Coating iron/steel with zinc to prevent corrosion; even if coating is scratched, Zn (above Fe in series) is sacrificed to protect Fe.
Calcination
Heating a metal carbonate or hydroxide strongly to decompose it; e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + H₂O.
Transition metal
d-block element with incomplete d-subshells; characterised by variable oxidation states, complex ion formation, coloured ions, and catalytic activity.
Complex ion / ligand
Complex ion: transition metal ion bonded to ligands; Ligand: molecule or ion that donates an electron pair to the metal (Lewis base); e.g. NH₃, CN⁻, H₂O, SCN⁻.
Solvay process
Industrial method for producing Na₂CO₃ from NaCl and CaCO₃ via ammoniated brine and NaHCO₃ precipitation; NH₃ is recycled; only NaCl and CaCO₃ consumed.
Wrought iron
Nearly pure iron (<0.1% C); soft, malleable, easily welded; made by puddling or removing carbon from pig iron; less used commercially now (replaced by mild steel).
